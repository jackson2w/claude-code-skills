# Human-made deck design

Will wants decks and visual assets that look like a thoughtful designer made them for this specific client, not like default AI output. Apply this before writing any slide, and run the audit at the end.

## 1. Get the real brand first

- Find the client's current brand before choosing any font or color: their live website (computed styles: font-family, heading and body colors, button colors), their current slide template, and their logo files.
- Check dates. Old templates in Will's folders can be outdated (example: AHP's 2016 PPT theme used Arial and an old wave logo; the 2025 brand is Poppins, navy #214A77, coral dot #ED5B3C, lowercase "ahp." logo).
- Prefer a logo file Will provides. Make light/dark variants (for example, recolor white to the brand navy) rather than placing a logo on a clashing background.
- If a formal brand guide may exist, say so and ask Will to get it from the client; the website is a fallback.
- Check contrast: a bright accent (like a coral) often fails 4.5:1 for small text. Use it for large text, shapes, and markers; pick a darker text-safe variant for small labels.

## 2. Avoid these AI tells

Visual and layout:
- Cream or beige backgrounds with muted brick-red accents (the default AI palette).
- Generic "editorial" serif-plus-sans pairings unrelated to the client's brand.
- Small uppercase, letter-spaced eyebrow labels above every heading.
- Colored accent bars on every block.
- Rows of identical rounded, thin-bordered cards on slide after slide.
- "01 / 02 / 03" numbering used as decoration.
- Italic pull quotes with a colored left border.
- Dashboard-style stat tiles giving many numbers equal weight; invented round numbers.
- Every slide with the same margins, density, and structure; no visual arc.
- "Diagrams" that are just a row of boxes with arrows.
- Decorative elements that carry no meaning; generic stock imagery.
- The same footer text repeated with no purpose.

Writing:
- Em dashes; "It's not X, it's Y"; reflexive groups of three; colon-titles everywhere; filler that could belong to any client.

## 3. Do this instead

- One typeface family (the brand's), hierarchy through weight and size. Four or five sizes, reused.
- A deliberate palette of about three brand colors plus neutrals; two background tones and one accent statement slide.
- Choose layout from content: plain lists separated by rules, real timelines, real cycle or process diagrams, split panels, a table when comparing pairs.
- Vary pacing: sparse dark dividers between sections, denser working slides in the middle, generous whitespace on statements.
- One brand-native motif (for example, the logo's dot as timeline markers or bullets), used sparingly and consistently.
- Quotes as large plain text with real quotation marks, separated by thin rules; note "unattributed, lightly trimmed" when applicable.
- One point per slide; titles state the topic in one consistent grammar.
- Logo on cover and close; a small logo plus one quiet footer line on content slides.
- Preserve Will's own edits and wording when revising; list any small text changes made.

## 4. Final audit

Before delivering, go slide by slide against section 2 and fix any tell found. Then tell Will:
- what was checked against the brand, with sources and a confidence score,
- which tells were removed,
- what could not be verified visually (positioned diagrams, connectors) and should be eyeballed,
- export caveats (for example, a Google font may substitute in PPTX on a machine without it; PDF keeps it).

Sources behind this checklist: Plus AI, "How to Make AI-Generated Slides That Don't Look Like AI Slop" (Dan Li, Jul 2026); Alai, "How to Make AI Slides That Actually Look Like a Designer Made Them" (Nandini Jain, Feb 2026).
